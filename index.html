VAST ARRAY

Everything we know about the universe arrived here as light. Not as samples, not as visits — as photons that left a long time ago and happened to land on a mirror. This is a short guide to reading them.

HOW A STAR BEGINS

Stars begin as cold gas. A molecular cloud sits at around ten kelvin and is dense enough to block the starlight behind it, which is why nebulae photograph as dark shapes at least as often as bright ones.

Where part of the cloud grows dense enough, gravity beats the outward push of gas pressure and the region falls inward on itself. Falling gas heats up. The core keeps collapsing and keeps heating until, at roughly ten million kelvin, hydrogen begins to fuse. The energy that releases pushes back exactly hard enough to stop the collapse.

That standoff — gravity pulling in, fusion pushing out — is a star. It will hold for as long as the fuel does.

THE LIFE OF A STAR

A star spends most of its life doing one thing: fusing hydrogen into helium and holding itself up with the energy that releases. Mass decides everything else about it.

Heavier stars burn hotter and far faster. A star ten times the Sun's mass has ten times the fuel and gets through it in about a thousandth of the time. When the hydrogen runs out the core contracts, the outer layers swell, and the star becomes a red giant.

What happens after that is mass again. A star like the Sun sheds its outer layers and leaves behind a white dwarf, cooling quietly for longer than the universe has existed. Above roughly eight solar masses the core collapses instead and the star detonates, leaving a neutron star or a black hole, and scattering the heavier elements it made. Every atom in you heavier than helium came out of one of those.

WORLDS AROUND OTHER STARS

We almost never see an exoplanet. We see what it does to its star.

A planet crossing in front of its star dims the light by a fraction of a percent, and does it again on a strict schedule. That is the transit method, and it has found most of what we know. A planet also pulls its star in a small circle as it orbits, which shows up as a rhythmic shift in the star's spectrum — the same effect that makes a passing siren change pitch.

Both methods favour big planets on short orbits, because those block more light and tug harder and repeat sooner. So the catalogue is badly skewed, and worth reading carefully: it is a map of what is easy to detect, not a census of what is out there.

EVERY IMAGE IS AN OLD IMAGE

Light is fast but it is not instant, so distance is delay. The Sun you can see is eight minutes old. Proxima Centauri is 4.24 years old. The galaxies in a deep-field exposure are billions of years old, and they are not where they appear to be — the universe kept expanding while their light was in transit.

Push far enough back and you reach the oldest light there is. Around 380,000 years after the Big Bang the universe finally cooled enough for atoms to hold together, and it went from opaque to transparent. The light released at that moment is still arriving, stretched by expansion out of the visible range and into microwaves. It is the cosmic microwave background, and it is everywhere you point.

There is nothing older to look at. Before that, there was nothing to see through.
